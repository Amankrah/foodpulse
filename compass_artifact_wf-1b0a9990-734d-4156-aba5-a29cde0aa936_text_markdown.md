# FoodPulse: Strategy Brief for a Reusable AI Skills System

## TL;DR
- FoodPulse is a solo-founder, evidence-based food-education brand run by McGill food-systems doctoral researcher Etornam C. Tsyawo, publishing deeply researched, illustration-rich articles across four pillars (Food and Wellbeing, Kitchen and Cooking, Food Literacy, Food Systems) on a Sanity-backed site, with a free monthly Kit newsletter, a tiered 1:1 coaching business, and a branded-but-empty YouTube channel that signals video is the next frontier.
- The single biggest leverage point is building three flagship AI skills that mirror the named pipelines (deep-research article production, on-brand illustration and data visualization, and article-to-YouTube repurposing), wrapped by a shared "house style" skill and a citation/fact-check skill, because the brand's whole value proposition is trustworthy YMYL content where one fabricated citation or off-brand visual destroys credibility.
- Sequence the build so the house-style and citation skills come first (they are dependencies for everything else), then the research-to-draft skill, then illustration, then the YouTube repurposing skill, then distribution skills (newsletter and social atomization); prioritize the article and repurposing skills for scale because they convert one researcher's time into many assets.

## Key Findings

### What FoodPulse is and how it operates
FoodPulse positions itself as "evidence-based food education" with the tagline "Nourish well. Live well." and the mission of "democratizing food knowledge." The About page states the goal plainly: "Help you understand food better so you can make informed decisions that support your health, your values, and your life," and frames the brand as a bridge between academic journals locked behind paywalls and clickbait headlines. The founder, Etornam C. Tsyawo, is described as a "Food Systems Research Engineer and Doctoral Researcher at SASEL Lab, McGill University" with a BSc in Chemical Engineering, an MSc in Food Science and Technology, and doctoral research in consumer food systems. Her research at McGill's Sustainable Agrifood Systems Engineering Lab has centered on food fortification in low- and middle-income countries through the FCI4Africa project. This credentialed-expert positioning is the brand's core asset.

The content is organized into four pillars: Food and Wellbeing (nutrition and health), Kitchen and Cooking (recipes and techniques), Food Literacy (labels and informed choices), and Food Systems (supply chains, sustainability, policy). The homepage counters show a still-early library of roughly 21 articles, 3 guides, 9 tools, and a glossary. The site runs on a Next.js front end with a Sanity CMS backing it (images are served from cdn.sanity.io).

The editorial voice is first-person, warm, and grounded in personal anecdote, then pivots to peer-reviewed evidence. A representative Food Systems article opens with the author's lived experience of moving from a food environment where healthy eating was normal to a college setting flooded with cheap soda, then layers in research citations before closing with practical, numbered strategies and a soft call to coaching. Articles follow a consistent structure: an evocative hook, a clear definition section, subheadings that pose reader questions, embedded infographics, numbered action lists, a "Sources and References" block linking primary research, an FAQ, tags, an author bio with credentials, and previous/next article navigation. The published editorial standards commit to reviewing peer-reviewed studies and WHO/FDA/NIH guidance, citing inline with reference lists, preferring primary sources, reviewing articles quarterly, and stamping a "Last updated" date.

The visual system is a signature differentiator. Each article carries a 1920x1080 cover image plus several custom infographics (portrait 800x1200 and landscape 1200x1000 formats recur), including data visualizations such as the okra article's nutritional-value-score bar chart and nutrient-breakdown panels. The visuals translate study findings into digestible graphics, which is exactly the kind of repeatable craft that an AI skill can systematize.

The business model is multi-pronged but early. All articles, tools, and the glossary are free. Revenue routes are a tiered 1:1 coaching practice (a $150 one-hour Food Clarity Session, a $520 three-week Personalized Food Systems Coaching package, and a $1,100 six-week Ongoing Mentorship limited to two spaces), digital products sold through a Kit storefront (an Instant Food Decision Guide at $39.99 and a goal-setting workbook at $9.99), a "Buy Me a Coffee"-style $5 tip jar, and an on-site Shop marked "Coming soon." The coaching copy is notably anti-template ("Not a diet programme. Not a meal plan"), emphasizing context, household, and durable decision-making frameworks, and explicitly distinguishes itself from clinical dietetics.

### Channels and distribution
The official site links exactly three off-site channels: the Kit newsletter, LinkedIn, and YouTube. There is no Instagram, TikTok, or X presence for this brand (an unrelated "thefoodpulse" food-discovery app and a placeholder @foodpulse Twitter handle should not be confused with it). The newsletter, titled "Food Decisions Simplified," is free and monthly, promising deep dives, subscriber-only recipes, quick tips, and early access to resources. The LinkedIn company page (foodpulseco) is the main social surface; the founder is active personally on LinkedIn, posting research-backed insights and stories. The YouTube channel (@foodpulsehq) is fully branded with a description and keyword set ("food education, nutrition facts, science-backed food facts, consumer food education... food system explained") but appears to be nascent with no discoverable published videos, which confirms the user's stated intent to move into video and means the repurposing skill will be building the pipeline from a near-blank slate rather than optimizing an existing one.

### The Canadian food-systems context
The timing is favorable. Health Canada's front-of-package labeling rules, finalized July 20, 2022, became mandatory on January 1, 2026 (the transition period ended December 31, 2025), with the Canadian Food Inspection Agency confirming no enforcement discretion beyond that date. The black-and-white magnifying-glass "high in" symbol generally triggers when a serving exceeds 15 percent of the daily value for saturated fat, sugars, or sodium. The scale of the underlying problem is well documented: Statistics Canada's Health Reports study (November 18, 2020) found that "just under half (46%) of total daily energy consumed by Canadians in 2015 came from ultra-processed foods" (median usual intake 44.9 percent), down slightly from 48 percent in 2004. International figures run higher; a 2024 BMJ umbrella review (Lane et al.) notes ultra-processed foods "can account for up to 58% of total daily energy intake in some high income countries," with the often-cited ~60 percent figure tracing to Martínez Steele et al. (2016 NHANES data, 57.9 percent of US dietary energy). Canada's Food Guide explicitly advises limiting highly processed foods. This regulatory shift, the NOVA-classification debate, and bilingual (English/French) labeling requirements give a Canadian food-literacy brand a steady stream of timely, locally relevant, high-search-interest topics. Comparable players in the broader landscape span evidence-based explainer channels (Nutrition Made Simple, Abbey the Food Scientist, NutritionFacts.org) and food-systems newsletters (Vittles, The Bittman Project, FSI's Field Notes, Unfork The Food System), most of which monetize via Substack/Kit subscriptions, sponsorship, and digital products. FoodPulse's defensible niche is the fusion of academic food-systems rigor with consumer-friendly translation and a strong house illustration style.

### What makes a good reusable agent skill
The Agent Skills standard was created by Anthropic and released as an open spec on December 18, 2025 (now hosted at agentskills.io), and has been adopted across more than 20 platforms including Claude Code, OpenAI Codex CLI, Gemini CLI, GitHub Copilot, Cursor, and VS Code. A skill is a folder containing a SKILL.md file with YAML frontmatter (name and description) plus optional references/, scripts/, and assets/ subfolders. The governing principle is progressive disclosure across three tiers: only the name and description (~100 tokens) load at startup; the full SKILL.md body (recommended under 5,000 tokens, with directories kept under roughly 500 lines) loads when the skill triggers; and deeper reference files load only when a task needs them. Per the spec and Anthropic's documentation, the name field maxes at 64 characters and the description at 1,024 characters. Best-practice guidance is consistent across sources: the description field is the trigger mechanism and should state both what the skill does and when to use it (slightly "pushy" wording reduces under-triggering); keep one skill to one job; keep references one level deep; prefer instructions over scripts unless determinism matters (validation, formatting, data parsing); and bundle templates and checklists as assets. Subjective work like writing style and design quality should be evaluated qualitatively by a human rather than forced into automated assertions.

Critically for a YMYL brand, the literature on AI citation hallucination is stark. The arXiv study "Detecting and Correcting Reference Hallucinations" (2026) reports that even a frontier model with web search access hallucinated about 30 percent of citations in hard multi-turn settings; the GhostCite benchmark (Xu et al., 2026) tested 13 LLMs across 40 domains and 375,000 citations and found hallucination rates ranging from 14 percent to 95 percent; and Liu et al. (2023) found that only 51.5 percent of generative-search sentences were fully supported by their cited sources. Every citation must therefore be verified against CrossRef/PubMed/Google Scholar and read for whether the source actually supports the claim. This is the single strongest argument for a dedicated, human-in-the-loop fact-check skill.

## Details: The Proposed Skills System

The recommended architecture is a small, composable set of skills rather than one monolithic "content" skill, because overlapping skills degrade selection and because FoodPulse's pipelines genuinely are distinct jobs. The skills fall into three tiers: foundation skills that everything else depends on, the three flagship production pipelines, and supporting distribution skills.

### Foundation skill 1: foodpulse-house-style
This is the keystone. It encodes the brand's editorial voice, values, and structural template so every other skill produces on-brand output. Its SKILL.md would capture the voice (first-person, warm, anecdote-then-evidence, empowerment over prescription, no fear-mongering or fad promotion), the four content pillars and how to classify a topic into one, the canonical article skeleton (hook, definition, question-subheads, infographics, numbered takeaways, sources block, FAQ, tags, author bio), formatting conventions, and the brand's non-negotiables ("we don't tell you what to eat," cite everything, say so when evidence is limited). It should bundle as references a voice guide with annotated good and bad examples pulled from existing articles, the pillar taxonomy, and a metadata/SEO template (title, meta description, keywords, slug, alt-text conventions). Every production skill references this one. Build it first because it is the dependency for all the others and because it captures the founder's tacit judgment in a reusable form.

### Foundation skill 2: foodpulse-citation-check
A dedicated fact-checking and citation-integrity skill, justified by both the YMYL nature of the content and the documented hallucination risk above. It would contain a verification workflow (extract every factual claim and statistic; identify the cited source; verify the source exists via DOI/CrossRef/PubMed; confirm the source actually supports the specific claim; flag mismatches; prefer primary research over secondary reporting), a checklist for distinguishing established consensus from emerging research, and rules for the "Sources and References" block and "Last updated" stamping. It pairs naturally with a small script for DOI/URL validation since that task benefits from determinism. This skill should run as a gate before any article publishes and before any video script is finalized. Build it second; it protects the brand's entire reason for existing.

### Flagship pipeline 1: foodpulse-deep-research-article
This is the highest-leverage production skill and the heart of the business. It orchestrates the full path from topic to publish-ready draft: clarify the angle and target pillar; run structured source gathering favoring peer-reviewed journals, WHO/Health Canada/NIH guidance, and primary data; synthesize findings into the house article skeleton; draft in the brand voice via the house-style skill; insert inline citations; and hand off to the citation-check skill before a human edit. It should bundle reference files for trusted source domains, a food-systems research-method primer (how to read a nutrition study, effect sizes versus relative risk, the NOVA classification and its controversy, how to handle conflicting evidence), and Canada-specific context (Food Guide, the 2026 front-of-package rules and the 15-percent daily-value trigger, bilingual considerations). The human-judgment boundary should be explicit: AI drafts and gathers, but the credentialed founder signs off on interpretation, nuance, and any health framing, which is also what protects E-E-A-T authorship signals. This skill converts the founder's scarcest resource (deep-research time) into volume, so it is the top scaling priority.

### Flagship pipeline 2: foodpulse-illustration-design
This skill systematizes the signature visual style so visuals stop being a bottleneck. It would encode the brand's image system (cover image at 1920x1080, recurring portrait 800x1200 and landscape 1200x1000 infographic formats), a visual style guide (palette, typography, icon style, chart conventions, how a study finding becomes a graphic), and a decision tree for choosing the right visual type (cover, concept illustration, infographic, data chart, comparison table). It would map each article section to candidate visuals and generate briefs or prompts for an image/infographic tool, with a data-accuracy checkpoint (verify every number in a chart against the source, an area all sources flag as requiring human review) and an accessibility checkpoint (contrast, legibility, alt text). Because design quality is subjective, the skill should produce drafts and structured briefs for human selection rather than claim final authority. This is a strong second scaling lever because visuals are labor-intensive and highly repeatable.

### Flagship pipeline 3: foodpulse-article-to-youtube
This skill addresses the explicitly stated growth goal and builds the video pipeline from near-zero. It would transform a published article into a complete video package: a script that re-architects the long-form piece for spoken delivery (one idea per scene, conversational pacing, a strong hook in the first eight seconds, problem framing, evidence, takeaways, and a call to action) rather than reading the article aloud; a storyboard that maps existing article infographics and new b-roll to script beats; an SEO-optimized title (primary keyword in the first 60 characters, benefit-driven, not clickbait); a 200-plus-word description with timestamps and source links carried over from the article; tag and chapter suggestions; and two or three thumbnail concepts with bold, mobile-legible text. It should reuse the citation-check skill so claims spoken on camera are as rigorous as the written ones, and reference the house-style voice guide adapted for spoken word. Bundle a script template, a metadata template, and a thumbnail-text guide. Given the channel is currently empty, also include a starter playbook for channel setup (about section, keywords, playlist architecture for the four pillars). This is the skill that opens an entirely new audience surface, so it is a high priority right behind the article skill.

### Supporting skill: foodpulse-newsletter
Encodes the monthly "Food Decisions Simplified" format on Kit. It would assemble an issue from the month's articles (deep-dive summary, a subscriber-only recipe, quick tips, early-access resources), apply the brand voice, and produce subject-line variants for A/B testing. Lower build priority because cadence is only monthly, but high value for owned-audience growth and eventual paid-tier or sponsorship monetization (Kit takes a smaller cut than Substack and supports paid newsletters, recommendations, and sponsorships).

### Supporting skill: foodpulse-social-atomize
Implements the content-atomization (Create Once, Publish Everywhere) pattern, turning one article or video into platform-native posts. Given the brand currently publishes only on LinkedIn, the skill should first specialize in LinkedIn (the founder's strongest surface: mini-essays, carousels, research-backed hooks) and be structured to extend to Instagram or a YouTube Shorts strategy later. It extracts "content atoms" (a stat, a myth-bust, a step, a quote) and packages them per platform with distinct hooks. Lower priority than the flagship pipelines but a cheap multiplier once the others exist.

### A note on tooling and skill hygiene
Keep each SKILL.md focused and under the recommended length (under 5,000 tokens, directories under ~500 lines), push detailed style guides and source lists into reference files, and use scripts only where determinism helps (citation/DOI validation, metadata formatting, chart-number verification). Maintain a deliberately small catalog with sharply differentiated descriptions so the agent selects the right skill. Develop iteratively: draft a skill, run it on a real article, watch where the agent misfires, and refine the description and instructions rather than over-engineering up front.

## Recommendations

Build in five staged waves, gated by quality benchmarks rather than calendar dates.

Wave 1 (foundation, build now): foodpulse-house-style and foodpulse-citation-check. These are dependencies for everything else and they encode the brand's trust moat. Benchmark to proceed: the house-style skill reproduces the structure and voice of three existing articles to the founder's satisfaction, and the citation skill correctly flags at least one deliberately planted bad citation in a test draft.

Wave 2 (core scaling lever): foodpulse-deep-research-article. This is the priority skill because it multiplies the founder's research time. Benchmark: it produces a publish-ready draft that needs only light human editing and passes the citation gate, measured across three topics spanning different pillars.

Wave 3 (visual throughput): foodpulse-illustration-design. Benchmark: it generates on-brand cover and infographic briefs that the founder accepts with minimal revision, and the data-accuracy checkpoint catches a planted wrong number.

Wave 4 (new audience surface): foodpulse-article-to-youtube, paired with the channel starter playbook. Benchmark: a complete video package (script, storyboard, title, description, thumbnails) from an existing article that the founder judges ready to record. Track early video click-through rate and retention; educational/how-to content typically averages 4 to 6 percent CTR (search-driven traffic can run 8 to 15 percent, browse traffic 2 to 4 percent), so if CTR sits below that range, iterate on titles and thumbnails before scaling output.

Wave 5 (amplification): foodpulse-newsletter and foodpulse-social-atomize. Benchmark: a monthly issue and a week of LinkedIn atoms assembled from existing content with minimal editing.

Thresholds that should change the plan: if video retention and subscriber growth prove strong after roughly ten videos, elevate the YouTube skill and add a dedicated Shorts skill. If coaching demand outpaces capacity, add a coaching-intake/triage skill. If the brand moves to a paid newsletter tier, upgrade the newsletter skill with a free-versus-paid content-split workflow. If a second writer or contractor joins, the house-style and citation skills become onboarding infrastructure, which raises their value further.

Across all skills, hold one line firmly: AI gathers, drafts, designs, and packages, but the credentialed founder owns interpretation, health framing, and final sign-off. That human-judgment boundary is not a limitation to engineer away; in a YMYL food-education brand it is the source of the trust that the whole business is built on, and it is what keeps E-E-A-T authorship and accuracy signals intact.

## Caveats
Several specifics could not be verified and should be confirmed directly. Exact subscriber counts for the newsletter, follower counts for the LinkedIn company page, and the YouTube channel's subscriber and video counts were not retrievable because those platforms block automated access; the YouTube channel appears empty based on the absence of any discoverable videos, but this could not be definitively confirmed. The homepage content counters (roughly 21 articles, a small number of guides and tools) reflect an early-stage library and will have grown. Pricing on the Kit storefront appeared in what looked like USD and may differ by region. The coaching prices and structure were read from the live site and may change. The Canadian ultra-processed-food share differs by source and year (Statistics Canada reported 44.9 percent median usual intake for Canadian adults in 2015, while higher figures in the high-50s percent reflect other countries and datasets), so use the Statistics Canada figure for Canada-specific claims. Finally, the specific AI tools to pair with these skills (image generators, video tools, citation validators) were not the focus here and should be selected based on the founder's budget and the quality benchmarks above; the skills themselves are deliberately tool-agnostic so they remain portable across compliant agent runtimes.
