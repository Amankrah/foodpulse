## A new mark on the front of the package

You walk down a Canadian grocery aisle in 2026 and something is different. On the front of an increasing number of packages, soup cans, frozen dinners, crackers, breakfast cereals, sliced cheese, there's a small black-and-white magnifying-glass icon. At the top it reads "High in," followed by a word: "sodium," "sugars," or "saturated fat." Sometimes two, occasionally all three. At the bottom: "Health Canada."

This is Canada's new front-of-package nutrition symbol. It became mandatory on January 1, 2026, after a transition period that ended at the close of 2025. For about five months now, products imported, manufactured, or packaged at retail in Canada have had to carry it when their nutrient content crosses a defined threshold.

The symbol is the most visible change to Canadian food packaging in a decade. It's also the part of the package most likely to be misread. Some shoppers read it as a "do not eat" warning. Others ignore it once they've seen it enough times. It is neither. It's information. What kind, and how to read it, is the practical question worth asking.

## What the symbol is, in plain terms

The "high in" symbol is a federal nutrition-labelling tool introduced by Health Canada to help shoppers identify pre-packaged foods that are high in [one or more of three nutrients many Canadians already consume more of than dietary guidance recommends](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/food-nutrition/nutrition-labelling/front-package.html): saturated fat, sugars, and sodium.

Visually it's a small black-and-white rectangle with a magnifying-glass graphic inside. Three text elements: the words "High in" at the top, the specific nutrient(s) named in the middle, and the words "Health Canada" at the bottom, signalling that the government, not the manufacturer, requires it.

It is **not** a Nutrition Facts table. The full table is still on the back of the package and still tells you the per-serving and per-100g detail. The "high in" symbol is a screening signal. It's designed to be read at arm's length while you're standing in the aisle, comparing two similar products.

## What triggers the symbol, and what doesn't

The trigger is a percentage of the **Daily Value (DV)**, the reference amount Health Canada sets for each nutrient per day. The standard threshold is **15% DV per serving**. If a serving of a pre-packaged food contains 15% or more of the daily value for saturated fat, sugars, or sodium, that nutrient triggers the symbol.

There are two important exceptions to the 15% rule.

- **Small packages**, products with a reference amount of 30 g or 30 mL or less, use a [lower threshold of 10% DV](https://gazette.gc.ca/rp-pr/p2/2022/2022-07-20/html/sor-dors168-eng.html). The reasoning is that small-serving foods (think pickles, salad dressing, single cookies, individually portioned breakfast cereals) are easy to eat more than one serving of, so the lower trigger reflects realistic intake.
- **Prepackaged main dishes** with a reference amount of 200 g or more (170 g for main dishes intended for children aged 1 to 4) use a **higher threshold of 30% DV**. The reasoning here is the inverse: a complete meal contributing more of a nutrient is expected, and the bar for "high in" should reflect what's high for a *whole-meal* portion.

Some foods are exempt from the symbol entirely for technical or definitional reasons. The display surface has to be at least 15 cm² for the symbol to apply at all, which excludes the smallest packaging. Certain whole-food categories are also exempt.

> ⚠️ **The serving size matters.** The symbol is calculated per serving as defined on the Nutrition Facts table, not per package. A small portion of a sweet, salty, or rich food may sit below the trigger even if eating the whole package easily clears it. The symbol is most informative when you read it alongside the serving size on the back.

A useful rule: when comparing two similar products on the shelf with comparable serving sizes, the symbol does what it was designed to do. It tells you at a glance which one is denser in one of the three nutrients. When comparing across categories with different serving sizes, the symbol tells you less.

## Why these three nutrients, and why now

Saturated fat, sugars, and sodium are not arbitrary picks. Canadian dietary intake data has, for years, shown average consumption of all three running above what Health Canada and the World Health Organization recommend. The clearest case is sodium: the average Canadian intake sits around [2,760 mg per day, against a recommended daily limit of 2,300 mg](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/publications/food-nutrition/sodium-intake-canadians-2017.html). Roughly 58 percent of Canadians aged one and older exceed those targets. For males aged 14 to 30, the figure is over 90 percent. Most of that sodium isn't coming from the salt shaker. Bakery products, mixed dishes, processed meats, cheeses, soups, sauces, and condiments together account for about half of it.

Added sugars and saturated fat follow a similar pattern: intake at the population level above the levels public-health bodies recommend, with most of the surplus coming from pre-packaged products rather than home cooking. That's the design rationale for the symbol. It sits where the nutrients enter the basket.

The regulation itself is older than the symbol's appearance on shelves suggests. Health Canada published the [final amendments to the Food and Drug Regulations in the *Canada Gazette*, Part II, on July 20, 2022](https://gazette.gc.ca/rp-pr/p2/2022/2022-07-20/html/sor-dors168-eng.html), as SOR/2022-168. The roughly three-and-a-half-year transition period that followed gave industry time to redesign packaging and use up existing label stock. As of January 1, 2026, [the Canadian Food Inspection Agency has stated there is no enforcement discretion](https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/implementation-plan): products that don't comply enter the agency's standard regulatory response process.

There is one nuance worth knowing. Pre-existing packaging is allowed to be sold through. That covers products imported, manufactured, or packaged at retail before January 1, 2026. That's why you'll continue to see some shelf-stable products without the symbol throughout 2026 even when they would otherwise be required to carry it. The packages were legally labelled when they entered the stream.

## Does this kind of label actually change behaviour?

This is where the evidence gets interesting. And uncertain.

The strongest Canadian-specific projection comes from [a 2023 modeling study published in *Frontiers in Nutrition* by Flexner and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2023.1158498). The team used the 2015 Canadian Community Health Survey dietary data combined with a packaged-food composition database, then modeled four substitution scenarios: what would happen if 30%, 50%, 70%, or 100% of Canadians switched to similar products carrying fewer "high in" symbols. Their projection: depending on how widely the substitution behaviour spreads, average daily sodium intake could drop by 73 to 259 milligrams, total sugars by 2 to 7 grams, with smaller effects on saturated fat. The associated public-health modeling estimated that 2,148 to 7,047 deaths from non-communicable diseases could be averted over the medium term, primarily from cardiovascular causes.

These are real numbers, but read them with care. The estimates come from a single modeling study, not from observed behaviour. The authors note their projection is deliberately **conservative**. It does not include any benefit from food manufacturers reformulating products to drop just under the threshold. In other countries with mandatory front-of-package labelling, that kind of reformulation has historically been one of the larger effects.

The broader international evidence is more mixed. A [2024 systematic review in *Nutrients* by Guo and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu16172819) compared five categories of front-of-package label: numerical (like the European GDA), colour-coded (Multiple Traffic Lights, used in the UK), warning labels (Chile's octagonal warnings and the Canadian magnifying-glass style), positive logos, and graded indicators (like Nutri-Score and the Australian Health Star Rating). Across studies of younger consumers, graded indicators, especially Nutri-Score, performed best for purchase intention and food choice. Warning labels showed inconsistent results, sometimes effective and sometimes not. The review notes most studies were conducted in virtual shopping environments rather than real-world stores, and didn't specifically address Canada's "high in" design.

So the Canadian-specific projection suggests modest but real population-level benefits if the symbol shifts behaviour even partially. Whether it will shift behaviour at that scale in real stores, not in modeled substitutions or virtual shopping experiments, is an open question. We will know more in 2027 and 2028, when post-implementation data starts to surface. Until then, the symbol is a tool. Early signs from modeling are encouraging. Real-world impact is still being measured.

## How to actually use the symbol

Read the "high in" symbol as one input into a decision, not as the decision itself.

1. **Use it for direct comparisons.** When two similar products sit next to each other (two crackers, two pasta sauces, two breakfast cereals), the symbol does what it was designed to do. The one without the symbol, or with fewer named nutrients on it, is on that specific dimension the less-dense product.
2. **Check the serving size.** The symbol is calculated against the Nutrition Facts table's serving size, which varies between brands. A product with the symbol at a 30 g serving and one without it at a 50 g serving may be much closer than the front of the package suggests. Flipping to the back for a quick comparison takes seconds.
3. **Don't read it as a verdict on the food.** A whole-grain bread "high in sodium" still provides fibre, B vitamins, and energy a soft drink doesn't. A cheese "high in saturated fat" provides protein and calcium. The symbol flags one attribute. The food has many.
4. **Pay attention to which nutrient is named.** "High in sugars" on a yogurt is a different signal than "high in sodium" on a soup. Context matters. If you're cutting back on one specific nutrient for a reason that matters to you, the symbol makes that nutrient visible. If you're not, the information is less directly useful.
5. **Don't expect every product to have the symbol immediately.** Old-label products are allowed to be sold through. If you see two seemingly identical products, one with the symbol and one without, the un-symbolled package may simply pre-date the deadline. That doesn't mean it's lower in the named nutrient.

The symbol's job is to make a piece of information easier to find. Whether you act on it, and how, depends on what you're trying to do with your food choices. Your overall pattern, your household, your taste, your budget, your time, none of that is on the package.
